MSCI World vs S&P 500: What is the difference?

MSCI World and the S&P 500 are two major equity market indexes, but they represent different parts of the global stock market.

The MSCI World Index is designed to represent large and mid-cap companies across developed markets. MSCI states that the index covers approximately 85% of the free-float-adjusted market capitalisation in each country it covers.

The S&P 500 is designed to represent the large-cap US equity market. S&P Dow Jones Indices describes it as a benchmark for US large-cap equities and says it covers approximately 80% of available US market capitalisation.

In simple terms:

MSCI World gives exposure across developed markets, while the S&P 500 concentrates on large US companies.

Which is more diversified: MSCI World or S&P 500?

MSCI World is more geographically diversified because it includes developed markets outside the United States.

The S&P 500, however, contains a large number of companies across different industries within the US economy. It therefore provides diversification within the US market rather than international diversification.

Diversification does not mean that an investment cannot fall in value. Both indexes are equity indexes and can experience substantial declines during periods of market stress.

Important distinction:

A portfolio can be highly diversified by company or sector while still being concentrated geographically.

MSCI World vs S&P 500 returns

One of the most common searches is "MSCI World vs S&P 500 returns". However, comparing returns requires care.

Returns depend on the exact period measured, the currency used, whether price or total returns are compared, and whether dividends are included.

For example, an investor comparing the indexes in pounds may see a different result from an investor comparing them in US dollars.

Why past performance should not be treated as a forecast

  • Markets can outperform or underperform for long periods.
  • Currency movements can affect returns for UK investors.
  • Dividends and reinvestment affect total returns.
  • Index composition changes over time.
  • Past performance does not guarantee future results.

For current index performance data, investors should use the official index providers rather than relying on historical figures copied from third-party websites.

MSCI World vs S&P 500: US exposure

The US is an important part of MSCI World because the United States is one of the developed markets represented by the index.

The S&P 500 takes the concentration further because it represents US large-cap companies only.

This means someone holding an MSCI World fund is not necessarily avoiding US shares. Instead, they are combining US exposure with exposure to other developed markets.

MSCI World vs S&P 500: Which has more companies?

The MSCI World Index and S&P 500 use different index methodologies, so simply comparing the number of constituents does not tell the whole story.

MSCI World includes large and mid-cap companies across developed markets, whereas the S&P 500 represents a broad cross-section of large US companies.

The exact number of constituents can change as indexes are reviewed and companies enter or leave the indexes.

MSCI World or S&P 500 for a UK investor?

There is no universal answer to whether MSCI World or the S&P 500 is better for every investor.

The decision depends on factors such as investment objective, risk tolerance, desired geographic exposure, investment timeframe, currency considerations and the rest of the investor's portfolio.

Someone considering MSCI World may value:

  • International diversification across developed markets.
  • Exposure to both US and non-US developed markets.
  • A broad global developed-market benchmark.

Someone considering the S&P 500 may value:

  • Focused exposure to large US companies.
  • A simple US equity benchmark.
  • Exposure to major US industries and businesses.

Neither index should automatically be considered "better". They are different tools for gaining equity-market exposure.

MSCI World vs S&P 500 ETFs

Investors cannot buy an index directly. Instead, they may use funds or exchange-traded products designed to track an index.

UK investors can find ETFs and index funds that track MSCI World or the S&P 500. Costs, tracking difference, fund structure, taxation, currency treatment and availability should be checked before investing.

The index and the fund tracking it are not the same thing. An ETF can have fees and tracking differences that affect an investor's actual return.

MSCI World vs S&P 500: risks to understand

  • Equity risk: both can fall significantly.
  • Market concentration: individual companies can have substantial index weights.
  • Currency risk: UK investors can be affected by exchange-rate movements.
  • Valuation risk: prices can become expensive relative to company fundamentals.
  • Economic risk: recessions and changing interest rates can affect equity markets.
  • Tracking risk: an ETF may not exactly match its index return.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is MSCI World better than the S&P 500?

Not necessarily. MSCI World and the S&P 500 have different geographic exposures and investment characteristics. Which is more appropriate depends on an investor's objectives and portfolio.

Does MSCI World include the S&P 500?

MSCI World does not contain the S&P 500 as an index. However, MSCI World includes US companies, many of which are also represented in major US indexes.

Is MSCI World more diversified than the S&P 500?

Geographically, yes. MSCI World covers multiple developed markets, while the S&P 500 is focused on the United States.

Which has had higher returns?

The answer depends on the period, currency and return measure being compared. Historical performance can change substantially depending on the dates selected.

Can UK investors buy MSCI World ETFs?

Yes. A number of investment funds and exchange-traded products are available to UK investors that seek to track MSCI World or similar developed-market indexes. Availability and suitability vary by fund.

Can I invest in both MSCI World and the S&P 500?

It is possible to hold funds tracking both indexes, but investors should understand that the two have overlapping US exposure. Holding both does not automatically provide twice the diversification.
